02 Workflow Automation

Automating manual, high-touch steps of the content delivery workflow

After launching the channel, the volume of titles we processed each month began to grow. But the existing supply chain could only scale by adding people. I identified the areas where automation could have the greatest impact, built a roadmap around them, and delivered the first automation feature—returning value immediately.

The problem

Delivery work was fragmented and required a lot of manual effort. As our catalog grew, Content Operations was spending more time on data entry, generating deliverables by hand, and coordinating work across a decentralized process. The existing workflow could scale, but mostly by adding more people.

The product opportunity

I saw an opportunity to turn the most repetitive parts of the workflow into software. The goal was to give Content Operations a more consistent process and free up their time for the work that actually required judgment.

My approach

Customer needs

I worked with Content Operations to map how content moved through the workflow and where time was being lost. Three areas stood out: manual data entry, manual deliverable generation, and a process spread across too many places.

Roadmap

I turned those findings into a phased automation roadmap and executive memo. Given our budget constraints, I focused on changes that could deliver value quickly rather than trying to automate the entire workflow at once.

Buy-in

I used Cursor to build working prototypes and put them in front of stakeholders early. It gave us something concrete to react to and helped us work through the details before committing engineering resources.

Implementation

The first feature automated avails generation, validation, and delivery by connecting directly to Amazon’s free Content APIs.

Evaluation

The feature saved more than the original target of 10 hours per week while staying within our guardrails of 99% SLA and less than a 5% delta in rejections.

Outcome

50 hours/month returned to Content Operations.

The first automation was intentionally small, but it established a repeatable approach: find the parts of the workflow that consume the most operational time, automate them one at a time, and measure whether the change actually makes a difference.
